\section{Introduction}
Nicolo Tartaglia was an Italian Renaissance mathematician, who was tasked with modeling the motion of cannonballs by the Doge of Venice in order to confer a military advantage. His predicted trajectory of a cannonball is shown in Figure 1.

[inster figure]

He also predicted the ranges for cannonballs shot at different angles, as shown in table 1. In this experiment, we aim to test his theories and see if they match predictions made by modern theories of physics.

Consider the horizontal and vertical components of projectile motion as given by Equations 1 and 2 where $v_0$ represents the initial velocity of the projectile, $\theta$ represents the initial angle, and $x_0$ and $y_0$ represent the initial positions on their respective axis.
\begin{equation}
  x(t) = v_0\cos{\theta}t + x_0
\label{Equation 1. Horizontal component of projectile motion} % Label
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  y(t) = v_0\sin{\theta}t-\frac{1}{2}gt^2 - y_0
\label{Equation 2. Vertical component of projectile motion} % Label
\end{equation}

In order to calculate the horizontal range, we need to compute when $y(t)=0$ via quadratic formula, and substitute the resulting $t$ value into $x(t)$.

Doing so, we get our final equation for which we fitted the data to.

\begin{equation}
  displacement = v_0\cos{\theta}* \frac{-v_0\sin{\theta} - \sqrt{v^2\sin{\theta}^2 - 4(y_0)(-\frac{1}{2}g)}}{-g}
\label{Equation 3. Model of horizontal displacement}
\end{equation}
